DeciBuy

Designing a calmer moment before a purchase.

A phone app that looks at a price, your spare money and your payday, and answers before you pay: Buy, Wait for payday, or Not now. Every list in it is a stack of folders you can open, flick and pull.

01 · The idea

Where did all that money go?

Many people I talk to don’t track their spending, or can’t keep it up for long. Sales, discounts and online stores make the monthly total very big, and then comes the question: where did I spend so much?

Before I buy a thing or tickets, I ask myself the same questions. How much can I afford it? How many hours of work is it? How will it hit me next month? How much further away does it push the goal I’m saving for? How often will I really use it? Few people will sit down and do that math before every purchase.

The principle

A product,and three answers.

The app doesn’t limit you, and the final call is always yours. This one line decided the visuals, the way the app talks to you, and the savings goal.

BuyWait for paydayNot now
02

Understanding the moment

What I knew, what I only simulated, and what each one changed.

Most of my research was simulated. I used AI to explore the problem quickly and to write hypotheses. None of it counts as evidence about real people, so every decision below is a hypothesis until it’s tested.

  • My own

    Observation and conversations with people at work, friends and relatives. Informal, not recorded.

  • Real

    Desk research and a competitor scan: Skip or Buy, CartPause, price trackers, budgeting apps.

  • Real

    5 survey responses in Google Forms. Too few to count; I don’t show them as numbers.

  • AI-simulated

    15 survey responses built from buyer types known in marketing and sales, and 5 interviews (P01 to P05). The personas, empathy maps and journey maps were built from these.

  • AI-simulated

    A card sort with 18 synthetic participants: an exploration of navigation options, not proof.

Three hypotheses, and what each one changed

Nobody wants another budget tracker.

Own observation · simulated
Observation
People around me stop tracking spending after a while. In the simulated sample, the reasons were effort, guilt, and income that changes every month.
So
Ask for as little as possible, and only once.
Decision
Setup asks for ranges once: income, rent, bills, payday. No spending log, no bank connection.

The decision is fast. The regret comes later.

Simulated interviews
Observation
In the simulated interviews, buying took minutes, often late at night, and regret came with the package or with overlapping installments.
So
The answer has to arrive in seconds, and come back later.
Decision
A verdict right after adding an item. Wait for payday comes with a date, a reminder and a fresh check on payday.

A yes matters as much as a no.

My principle · simulated
Observation
The app shouldn’t judge or pressure. In the simulated interviews, people wanted to hear “yes, buy it” too.
So
The same rules for every answer.
Decision
Buy is a real answer. I dropped a “be strict with me” mode: a verdict that changes with what you want to hear isn’t a verdict.

The name

It started as Impulse Check.

The old name said that purchases are only impulsive. But not all purchases, and not all people, are about impulse. Some want to weigh how much a buy is worth. So the app was named from Decision and Buy, and clearer answers came from that: buy, wait, or not now. It won’t judge you, won’t pressure you, won’t tell you you’re deciding wrong.

Finding the interaction

From rows to folders: where the idea came from, what I rejected, and why it stayed.

Step 1Exploration

In my Crazy 8’s for My items, most sketches stack the items. The folder idea started here, before any screen existed.

How Might We, and the graph detail round
Step 2Selection
Evidence

A template list

Low-fi v1 was made with AI in Figma: lists, item rows with a square thumbnail, boxed fields. Nothing says this is one item you opened.

Evidence

My folders

For v2 I redesigned onboarding and Home myself and set one graphic language for the whole app. The verdict is an open folder, and the next item waits below it.

Product

Color is the verdict

The folder takes the color of its answer, the reason line gives a date and what you’d have left, and the facts sit below in the same order for every answer.

Why folders, not rows. An item opens like a folder and the rest slide down, so you never lose the list. The color is the verdict, so a stack reads without words. The tab shows the price, so you never guess what something costs.

Who did what at this stage

I designed

Onboarding and Home in low-fi v2, the visual language, the palette and app icon, the logo.

Claude produced

Low-fi v1, then v2 applied to the other screens, the hi-fi screens and the design system, through the Figma connection.

I rejected and changed

Its first pass drew plain lists with thumbnails. I pointed to Recent checks on Home: every list became a folder stack, the item page an open folder. I removed the “+” on Check and the verdict word on folder tabs.

“Look at my updated design. It’s low-fi, don’t nitpick colors. Build the remaining screens according to this design.”

My prompt in Figma, 2026-10-18. Paraphrased: I dictated it by voice in Russian, and the exact wording wasn’t saved.

An image model tried too, and lost the folders

I asked ChatGPT for three grayscale alternatives per screen, based on my Crazy 8’s. It came back with a generic fintech look: lists, grey boxes, the verdict in a box instead of owning the screen. Its “stacked folders” version lost the folder idea. I kept a few details: the Check empty state that explains the rules, the “as of” date on saved prices, sort as a bottom sheet.

Step 3The language around it
Product

A companion, not a judge. Even its red shouldn’t cause anxiety.

“Not now” doesn’t say “don’t buy”. It doesn’t forbid you. It says lightly: some day you can, keep saving, but not now. The rounded corners soften the tone, so nothing feels sharp or fixed.

  • Removed

    Product photos. A photo pulls you to the “want”; the screen is about what this price means for you.

  • Removed

    The verdict word on folder tabs. With several folders you saw “Buy” repeated and no price. Now every tab shows the price.

  • Removed

    A light grey background. A contrast check showed the light verdict folders would blend into it.

  • Removed

    The hamburger menu. It would hold only Settings, so a gear is one step shorter.

Step 4Structure

I didn’t drop the wishlist. I moved it into Check and limited it to 5 items for 3 days.

On purpose, so it doesn’t turn into one more cart you never finish. The hamburger went too. Everything else fits in four tabs: Home, Check, My items, My money.

Evidencev03, after the Crazy 8’s: Check becomes its own tab with drafts; the four tabs that shipped.

Between v01 and v02 I ran a simulated card sort (18 synthetic participants). I read it as an exploration of options, not as proof that the structure works.

04

The product in motion

I wanted control over continuous motion, gesture speed and springs you can interrupt, so I prototyped in code instead of in Figma. I described each motion with references, and it went straight into code.

Gesture lab

Try the folders yourself.

  1. Tap a folderIt opens, and the others slide down below it.
  2. Grab it while it movesThe spring follows your finger from wherever it is.
  3. Flick or drag itThe spring keeps the speed of your finger.
  4. Pull up the stack under an open folderThe rest rise so you can see and switch.

The real prototype on its demo data; nothing is saved. The buttons are a way in without gestures. Screen reader support for the gestures is still open.

Verdict explorer

Three outcomes, one rule.

Same layout, same facts, same rules. Only the color, the word and the reason line change. Examples from the prototype’s demo data.

Buy

A $39 sneaker cleaning kit. “Fits your month.” The first tile says how comfortable it is: 7.8% of your spare money.

Wait for payday

A $180 lamp, or 4 × $45. “Payday is in 4 days. Buy it now and you’ll have only about $160 until then.” The app judges the full price, not the monthly payments.

Not now

A $1,240 camera. “That’s about 2½ months of your spare money.” It doesn’t forbid; it says some day you can.

What never changes: the item on top, the reason line, the fact tiles in the same order, and the same actions under the folder. Behind each tile there’s one graph and one plain sentence, one swipe away.

Every key screen

One decision per screen.

31 screens in Figma, light and dark. These six carry the system. Tap one to open it with the decision behind it.

What the code taught me: the keyboard covering a field on a small iPhone, scrolling cut short on iOS, and action links that did nothing in German were all invisible in a desktop browser.

Testing and iteration

Three kinds of checks, kept apart, because they are worth different things.

  • Real people

    Two friends, informally. F1 tested over chat, sending screenshots and questions as she went: 11 points. F2 asked about currencies (recalled, not written down). No tasks, no recordings.

Four changes that came from testing

The verdict forgot what I had bought

My iPhone walk-through

Before
After I had spent all my spare money, a $50 T-shirt still said Buy. The verdict looked at the monthly budget.
After
Verdicts follow the money left until payday, and show how far over budget you are, in red.
Why
A verdict that ignores what you already did can’t be trusted twice.

“You don’t believe in my expenses enough”

F1

Before
Bills topped out at “$700+”, so $720 and $3,000 were the same. Only income had an exact amount.
After
An exact amount for bills too. It opens by itself on “$700+”.
Why
I hadn’t carried a pattern to the step that needed it. Ranges stay the default.

Dollars only

F1 · F2

Before
A global product tested on a US example. F1 mixed shekels and dollars in one setup.
After
The currency comes from the phone’s region; each item can have its own, converted with “≈”.
Why
Wrong money in means a wrong verdict out.

“Can I really do this?”

F1

Before
The prototype showed Share from a store and Paste link, which don’t work yet. Every link failed and read as broken.
After
“Coming soon” on Share, Paste link and the onboarding step, with no change to the layout.
Why
I had shown the planned product, not what works today.

What happened to F1’s 11 points

Accepted and built

  • Exact amount for bills
  • Edit a name or a price
  • Currencies
  • Coming soon on Share and Paste link

Decided, not built yet

  • Goal progress: at payday the app asks you to confirm what you saved
  • Where the money is kept: a copy question, still open

Kept as designed, still open

  • Drafts. Only a real person asked what they are for; I kept them, and the wording needs a real test

Not reproduced

  • Digits hidden while typing: not seen on my phone. I added a keyboard guard; not yet retested on a small iPhone
06

Reflection

Limitations

  • The interviews and 15 of 20 survey responses were simulated, so the personas and journey maps are hypotheses.
  • Real users so far: two friends, informally.
  • Screen reader support for the gestures is open.
  • Sharing from a store isn’t built.

Needs real-world validation

  • Whether people find that the stack can be pulled, without being told.
  • Whether a verdict feels fair, and people come back on payday.
  • Whether drafts make sense to someone who didn’t design them.

What I’d do differently

  • Test with real people earlier. Hi-fi was ready on Oct 19; real people came later and informally.
  • Test on a real iPhone and in other languages from the first coded version.

What I learned

The designer stays the author.

This is my second project as a product designer, and I feel more confident with every decision. A designer won’t do everything by hand, but will direct the whole process: see the app as a user, a developer, a designer and a marketer, keep all of it in mind, check which decisions work, and run stress tests.

AI is becoming a key tool for the whole product, not only the design. In this project it built screens from my direction, simulated research I then had to treat as hypotheses, and ran stress tests. The design decisions, and what I rejected, were mine.
