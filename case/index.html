01 · The challenge

Where did all that money go?

Many people I talk to don’t track their spending, or can’t keep it up for long. Sales, discounts and online stores make the monthly total very big, and then comes the question: where did I spend so much?

Before I buy a thing or tickets, I ask myself the same questions. How much can I afford it? How many hours of work is it? How will it hit me next month? How much further away does it push the goal I’m saving for? How often will I really use it? I heard the same questions from people at work, friends and relatives. Many care about this and have no clear answer. And few people will sit down and do the math before every purchase.

Who it hurts

People who earn their own money and buy online, often in the evening, fast, from a feed, an ad or a sale.

What they do today

Leave it in the cart, take a screenshot, compare prices. Some tried a budgeting app and stopped: too much logging, or it made them feel guilty.

My goal

A clear answer at the moment of buying, from facts about your own money. No expense tracking, no bank login, no judging.

02 · Research

What I asked, and whom

Desk research and a competitor scan first (Skip or Buy, CartPause, price trackers, budgeting apps). Then a survey in Google Forms: 20 responses, 18 qualified. Then five one-on-one interviews, P01 to P05. The interviews built two groups, and each group became a persona.

About the survey sample

15 of the 20 survey responses are synthetic: I built them from buyer types known in marketing and sales, and mixed them with the real answers. I analysed it as one sample, so the counts below show a direction, not proof.

01

Speed predicts regret

Of 11 people who paid within an hour, 9 barely used the thing. Of 6 who took a day or longer, 5 use it weekly. So the app’s main answer became a pause with a reason and a date: Wait for payday.

02

Money apps fail when the model doesn’t fit life

8 of 13 who tried a money app stopped: effort, guilt, income that changes every month. So DeciBuy asks for ranges once, never asks you to log spending, and works with biweekly and irregular pay.

03

Permission matters as much as warning

Three of five interviewees wanted to hear “yes” too. So Buy is a real answer, given by the same rules as the other two, not a reward for saying no.

Stop me. But nicely. Not like my mom.P01, interview, 22, shift worker

The finding that renamed the product

The project started as Impulse Check. Research showed that was too narrow.

The old name said that purchases are only impulsive. But not all purchases, and not all people, are about impulse. Some want to weigh how much a buy is worth. So the app was named from Decision and Buy, and clearer answers came from that: buy, wait, or not now. The app doesn’t limit you. The final call is yours, and the app says so on every verdict. It won’t judge you, won’t pressure you, won’t tell you you’re deciding wrong.

The survey also suggested two modes, “help me feel sure” and “be strict with me”. I dropped that: a verdict that changes with what you want to hear isn’t a verdict. The rules stay the same for everyone.

That is why saved items don’t just sit in a list. Wait for payday comes with a date and a reminder, and on payday the item is checked again with the new numbers.

Information architecture

I didn’t drop the wishlist. I moved it into Check and limited it to 5 items for 3 days.

On purpose, so it doesn’t turn into one more cart you never finish. In Check you keep only the items you’re unsure about, before you decide to track them in My items. The hamburger menu went too: the only thing in it would be Settings, so Settings is one tap away instead. Everything else fits in four tabs: Home, Check, My items, My money. A simulated card sort (18 synthetic participants) put Settings outside the main tabs in every run.

The principle

A product,and three answers.

This one line decided the visuals, the way the app talks to you, and the savings goal: if you save for something, the app reminds you of it, so you don’t forget what you’re saving for.

04 · Design language

A companion, not a judge

The app must never make you feel shame. Even its red shouldn’t cause anxiety.

I wanted the app to feel like a friend that helps you understand how much you can afford something: welcoming, so you come back for advice without being afraid of what you want to buy. Even the wording follows this. “Not now” doesn’t say “don’t buy”. It doesn’t forbid you. It says lightly: some day you can, keep saving, but not now.

Calm

One reason line on top, facts below. The same layout for all three answers, only the color changes.

Soft

Rounded corners everywhere, so nothing feels sharp or fixed. They soften the tone of the decision.

Bright, but clean

Strong colors on a white ground. Each color has one meaning and the brand blue never touches the verdict colors.

Facts first

No product photo. A photo pulls you to the “want”; the screen is about what this price means for you.

Manrope

05 · Screens and the live app

One decision per screen

Each caption is the decision behind the screen, not a description of it. The screens follow the page theme: switch it at the top to see dark mode.

The folders

Every item opens like a folder and the rest slide down. That’s why the prototype is code, not Figma.

Figma can’t do these gestures: grab a folder mid-motion, flick it, pull the stack up to see what’s under it. The live app below is the real prototype. Add an item, open a folder, raise the stack. Data stays on your device.

06 · Working with AI

Where it helped, and where I had to stop it

The design and the visuals are mine. With Claude I built a system and made screens faster, and then edited them to match how I wanted them to look.

I designed onboarding and Home myself in Figma, which set the visual language. Then Claude applied it to the remaining screens through the Figma connection, built the hi-fi from my color board and set up the design system (Light and Dark variables, text styles, components). I reviewed every round.

What it did well

Speed and consistency: 31 screens in light and dark from my components, overflow checks across every frame, contrast checks before I decided on colors.

Where it was wrong

On the first pass it ignored my folders and drew plain lists with a thumbnail, the layout carried over from the first low-fi rows. It added a “+” on Check, which already is the add screen. In the design system every folder tab said “Buy”.

How I fixed it

I pointed to Recent checks on Home: every item list became a folder stack and the item page became an open folder. I removed the “+”, the thumbnails and the verdict word on tabs. Color is the verdict.

The prompt that moved it

Look at my updated design. It’s low-fi, don’t nitpick colors. Build the remaining screens according to this design.My prompt to Claude in Figma, 2026-10-18 (paraphrased from a Russian voice message)
Marked gap

I don’t have the exact wording: the prompt was dictated by voice. What changed the result was showing my own screens as the reference instead of describing the style in words.

The image model, earlier

ChatGPT was useful for edge states and small details, not for the concept.

I asked it for three grayscale alternatives per screen, based on my Crazy 8’s sketches. It came back with a generic fintech look: lists, grey boxes, the verdict sitting in a box instead of owning the screen. Even its “stacked folders” version lost the folder idea. I kept a few details: the Check empty state that explains the rules, the “as of” date on saved prices, sort as a bottom sheet.

07 · Design to code

Straight from motion references to code

I skipped Figma prototyping on purpose: some of these interactions can’t be built there. I described each motion with references and it went straight into code.

It isn’t my first project, so moving from design to a live prototype wasn’t the hard part. The prototype is one HTML file on GitHub Pages: spring physics for the folders, data kept on the phone, light and dark, six languages, installable on the home screen.

What worked

Folders you can grab mid-motion and flick, the same money math on every screen, and real verdicts from my rules (verdict logic v05), not fixed demo screens.

What broke

Things I couldn’t see in a desktop browser: the keyboard covering the field on a small iPhone, scrolling interrupted on iOS, and action links that did nothing in German, because the code read the translated text.

What I’d do differently

Test on a real iPhone and in other languages from the first coded version, not after many versions.

For a future launch I also benchmarked a product page parser on 183 links from 10 stores. It’s groundwork for releasing the app; it doesn’t change the design shown here.

08 · Before and after

From a template to my folders

The first low-fi was a generic list. The folder idea came from my own Crazy 8’s sketches.

Low-fi v1 was made with AI in Figma: lists, item rows with a square thumbnail, boxed fields. For v2 I redesigned onboarding and Home myself and set one graphic language for the whole app, so nothing is out of balance: Bricolage titles, Manrope text, folder stacks, rows that run off the screen edge like tabs on sheets. The final version adds color, depth and real data.

09 · Testing

Three kinds of testing, three kinds of finds

I ran 20 synthetic AI testers on the prototype, then watched myself use it on my iPhone, then gave it to friends. Every remark was fixed: theirs, my friends’, and my own.

20

Synthetic testers

AI agents, each with a persona and a focus: tight budget, irregular income, huge numbers, an older user, accessibility, gesture chaos. A wide run of the app through many scenarios. They found the bugs: names rendered as code, $7.99 becoming $799, double taps creating duplicates.

9

Fixes from my own video

A 9-minute walk-through on my iPhone. The biggest find: after I had spent all my spare money, a $50 T-shirt still said Buy. Verdicts now follow the money left until payday.

11

Points from one friend

She tested over chat, sending screenshots and questions as she went. A second friend asked what happens if your money isn’t in dollars.

Synthetic testers found the bugs. Only a real person asked: what’s the point of drafts?

She also asked “can I really do this?” about sharing from a store, which the prototype promised but couldn’t do. That became Coming soon labels. Currencies came from the friends too: the app now takes the currency from your phone’s region and converts per item.

Who was measured

The 20 testers are simulated, not people. The real users so far are two friends, informally, with no tasks or recordings. A scripted test with 4 to 5 people on the current version is still to do.

What I learned

Designer as director

This is my second project as a product designer, and I feel more confident with every decision. I see more and more that a designer won’t do everything by hand, but will be the director, the scriptwriter and the manager of the whole process: see the app as a user, a developer, a designer and a marketer, keep all of it in your head, check which decisions work and run stress tests.

AI is becoming a key tool for the whole product, not only the design. For a designer without a team it’s a big help: it widens what you can do around the product.

What I’d do differently

Real people, real phones, earlier

  • Test with real people earlier. Hi-fi was ready on Oct 19; real people came later and informally. Synthetic testers found bugs, but only a person asked what drafts are for.
  • Test on a real iPhone and in other languages from the first coded version.
